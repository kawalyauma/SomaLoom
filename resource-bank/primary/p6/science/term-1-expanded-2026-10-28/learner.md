## 1. Mammals: corrected groups and examples

Mammals are vertebrates whose females produce milk for their young. Most have hair at some stage. A four-chambered heart separates oxygen-rich from oxygen-poor blood. Most give birth to live young, but platypuses and echidnas lay eggs. Primates include monkeys and human beings. Rodents include rats, mice and porcupines; their front cutting teeth keep growing. Hoofed mammals include cows and goats. Carnivora is a group including dogs, cats and lions, although not every member eats only meat. Bats belong to Chiroptera and are the only mammals capable of sustained powered flight. Marsupials include kangaroos and koalas; their young are born at an early stage of development and often develop in a pouch. Monotremes include platypuses and echidnas. Rabbits, hares and pikas are lagomorphs, not rodents. Whales and dolphins are cetaceans, but seals are pinnipeds, not cetaceans. Hedgehogs and elephant shrews are not in one natural 'insectivore' group under modern classification.

## 2. Birds, reptiles, fish and amphibians

Birds are warm-blooded vertebrates covered in feathers, with toothless beaks; all living birds lay eggs. Examples of birds of prey are eagles and hawks. Ducks are adapted to swimming; pigeons perch; parrots can climb; flamingos wade; chickens scratch the ground; ostriches cannot fly. These are useful behavioural groupings, not separate formal classes. Reptiles, including snakes, lizards, tortoises and crocodiles, have dry scaly skin, breathe with lungs and rely on their environment to regulate body temperature. Most fish such as tilapia and Nile perch breathe through gills; fins help them move. Frogs, toads and newts are amphibians; many change from water-living larvae to adults that can live on land. Tadpoles commonly have gills; adult frogs use lungs and moist skin for gas exchange.

## 3. Arthropods, molluscs and worms

Invertebrates are animals without a backbone. Arthropods have jointed legs and an external skeleton. Adult insects have a head, thorax, abdomen and six legs; examples are houseflies, bees and butterflies. Housefly body diagram: HEAD (eyes, antennae, mouthparts) — THORAX (three pairs of legs and one pair of wings) — ABDOMEN. Spiders have eight legs and are arachnids; crabs are crustaceans. Molluscs include snails and slugs with soft bodies. Earthworms are segmented worms and help aerate soil; leeches are also segmented worms. Tapeworms and roundworms are different groups and can be parasites. Do not handle unknown worms, snails or insects without adult supervision.

## 4. Animal care and conservation

Protect domestic animals by giving suitable food, clean water, safe housing and veterinary care. Wild animals are important in food chains, pollination, seed dispersal and the balance of ecosystems. Avoid destruction of nests and habitats. Do not touch wild animals, suspected rabid mammals or unknown snakes; report an animal bite to an adult and seek urgent medical help.

## 5. Sound: production and properties

Sound is produced by vibrating objects. A drum skin vibrates when struck; a plucked string moves backwards and forwards; human vocal folds vibrate when we speak. Pitch describes how high or low a sound is and mainly depends on frequency: faster vibration gives higher pitch. Loudness depends chiefly on amplitude: greater vibration generally makes a louder sound. A short taut string usually produces higher pitch than a longer loose string under similar conditions. Sound needs a medium; it travels through gases, liquids and solids but not through empty space. A reflected sound heard distinctly is an echo. Approximate speed of sound in room-temperature air is 343 metres per second; speed varies with medium and temperature. Never make extremely loud sounds near ears.

## 6. Sound in living things, instruments and recording

People produce voiced sounds using vibrating vocal folds. Birds produce calls using a sound organ called the syrinx; insects may rub body parts together or vibrate wings. Instruments may be grouped by how sound is made: drums and xylophones are percussion instruments; guitars and violins have vibrating strings; flutes and trumpets use vibrating air columns. Sound recording stores a changing signal representing sound, so it can be played again: a microphone changes vibrations into an electrical signal, a recorder stores the signal, and a loudspeaker changes electrical energy back into sound. Modern phones usually store digital sound files. Avoid recording people without permission.

## 7. Structure, function and care of the ear

Sound pathway: outer ear/pinna → ear canal → eardrum → tiny middle-ear bones → cochlea → auditory nerve → brain. The pinna gathers sound. The eardrum vibrates; three middle-ear bones (hammer, anvil and stirrup) transmit vibrations. The cochlea is a fluid-filled inner-ear organ containing sensory hair cells that convert vibration into nerve signals. The auditory nerve carries these signals to the brain, which interprets them as sound. The semicircular canals help with balance, not hearing. Protect ears from loud noise, never insert sharp objects, and consult a health worker for pain, discharge, sudden hearing loss or ringing.

## 8. Circulatory system and heart anatomy

The circulatory system includes heart, blood and blood vessels. The heart is a strong muscular pump with four chambers: right atrium and ventricle, left atrium and ventricle. Valves prevent backflow. Correct labelled blood-flow diagram: BODY → venae cavae → RIGHT ATRIUM → RIGHT VENTRICLE → PULMONARY ARTERY → LUNGS → PULMONARY VEINS → LEFT ATRIUM → LEFT VENTRICLE → AORTA → BODY. The right side sends oxygen-poor blood to lungs; the left sends oxygen-rich blood to the body. The pulmonary artery is an important exception to the idea that arteries always carry oxygen-rich blood.

## 9. Blood vessels, blood components and circulation health

Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins towards the heart, and capillaries exchange materials with tissues. Artery walls are generally thick and elastic; many veins contain valves. Plasma carries dissolved substances. Red blood cells contain haemoglobin and carry oxygen; white blood cells defend against infection; platelets help clotting. Blood delivers oxygen and nutrients and transports waste products. Anaemia may reduce the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. Sickle-cell disease is inherited and affects haemoglobin. Leukaemia is a cancer affecting blood-forming tissues. A balanced diet and medical care support health, but these illnesses do not all have the same cause or treatment.

## 10. HIV, prevention and blood safety

HIV is the human immunodeficiency virus. It attacks important immune-system cells. AIDS is a condition that may develop when HIV severely damages the immune system, especially without treatment. A person with HIV can look healthy. HIV is spread by certain body fluids including blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk, but not by handshakes, sharing desks, food, coughs or mosquitoes. Prevention includes not sharing needles or razors, screened blood for transfusion, and access to HIV testing, counselling and treatment. Antiretroviral medicines help people with HIV lead long lives and reduce transmission. Do not stigmatise others. Never touch blood directly; seek trained adult help when someone is bleeding.

## 11. Alcohol, smoking, essential and traditional medicines

Alcohol is produced when yeast ferments sugars; it affects the brain, judgement and coordination. Alcohol dependence may disrupt personal health, families and community life. Active smoking is smoking tobacco; passive smoking is breathing tobacco smoke made by others. Both harm health. A drug is a substance that changes how the body functions; medicines are drugs used to prevent, diagnose or treat illness. Essential medicines are those selected to meet priority healthcare needs; being essential does not mean they are suitable for everyone. Preventive medicines include vaccines; other medicines may relieve symptoms or treat disease. Traditional remedies are prepared using local knowledge, but their safety, strength and purity may not be established. Laboratory-manufactured medicines also need correct instructions and quality checks. A prescription is a health worker's written direction about a medicine. Before using medicine, confirm the correct patient, drug, dose, timing and route with a qualified health worker. Misuse includes taking another person's medicine or using the wrong dose. Drug dependence is a strong need to continue use. Never make or taste alcohol, smoke, or experiment with tablets as a classroom activity.
